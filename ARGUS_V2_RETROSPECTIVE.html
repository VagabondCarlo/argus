In building a trading assistant, we learned valuable lessons about running a fully automated system safely — how to make it recover from its own failures, protect itself with hard limits on how much it could lose, and prove its results with real data instead of guesswork. But we ultimately found the tool is unable to reliably pick trades that make money in markets this closely watched by everyone else.

Overview
What Argus Was

The goal was simple: build a computer program that watches the stock and cryptocurrency markets, decides on its own when to buy and when to sell, and makes money doing it — without a person making each decision. It would run by itself, all day and all night, and only tell a person what it did after the fact.

Argus v1 tried this first and ran into problems before the real idea could even be tested: a bug made it buy far more than it should have, and then a separate bug made it stop trading almost immediately — it stayed stopped for three weeks without anyone noticing. v2 was a rebuild meant to fix those problems so the real question could finally get a fair test: can a program like this actually find trades that make money?

The rebuild kept the same three-part design but replaced almost everything else: the program protected its own trades in software instead of trusting the broker to do it, it kept score on trades it chose not to make so we could tell later if being pickier would have helped, and a second computer watched the first one and could fix it automatically if it broke.

It ran for 21 days. The engineering worked — it kept itself running, protected its own money, and recovered from its own failures without anyone stepping in. The trading did not make real money. This is the honest account of both.

System Design
Three Services, Two Machines, One Machine Watching the Other

Argus v2 kept v1's three-service split, self-hosted on a Mac Mini (Agent 1) with a second Mac Mini (Agent 2) providing LLM inference. What changed was what happened when something went wrong.

Service 1
Analyst
Scanned stocks, forex, metals, and crypto every 5 minutes (15 overnight), scored each snapshot technically (RSI, EMA, MACD, Bollinger, ATR), then ran every BUY/SELL candidate through a local LLM committee before saving. Self-healing: a chronic yfinance cache corruption is detected and the process re-execs itself rather than looping silently.
Service 2
Executor
Polled signals, ranked each fresh batch by confidence, and filled open slots best-first. Placed Alpaca orders and ran a 24/7 position monitor that enforces stop and target in software — the one piece of protection fractional and crypto positions actually have.
Service 3
Bot
Telegram owner commands, a guardrailed guest chat (input/output screening, 3-strikes mute), and a track-record feed that posted every close — built and tested, kept in preview the entire run pending a track record worth publishing.

Agent 2 became a guardian, not just a helper. A launchd probe checked Agent 1 every 5 minutes with an escalation ladder — silent retry, then remote restart plus a Telegram alert, then a machine-failure alarm. A second job replicated the database every 10 minutes with an integrity check before promotion. A warm-standby clone of the full stack sat ready on Agent 2 at all times. Failover itself stayed deliberately human-gated — two nodes sharing one brokerage account makes automatic failover a way to double-order, not a safety feature.

Development History
21 Days in Sequence
Jul 16
v2 goes live
Threshold at 0.72, ranked batch selection, software stops all wired. v1's known bugs — position sizing, the dead execution loop — fixed at the source.
Jul 17
First live proof of the crypto path
ADA-USD SELL crossed threshold at 02:23 ET and executed correctly — the first end-to-end confirmation the pipeline actually worked, not just backtested.
Jul 19
Security hardening + Agent 2 becomes guardian
Full git-history rewrite scrubbed every private IP from 101 commits; confirmed zero credentials ever committed. Same night: Agent 2 wired up as guardian + replica + warm standby, and the analyst became self-healing after a 5.5-hour cache-corruption stall proved manual fixes weren't enough.
Jul 20
Trend veto — the single biggest edge fix
Backtest of 98 trades: counter-trend BUYs won 26%, with-trend BUYs won 71%. Entering against the EMA trend was the system's main bleeder. Blocking it was the one change with a real, measured effect.
Jul 22–24
Unstarving the flow, shorting shipped
A silently dead stock feed plus a 0.66 floor had left only 3 eligible signals in 3.5 days — fixed and dropped to 0.64. Short selling built, tested (53 green), and enabled the same week. It would go on to never fire once in the live run.
Jul 29
The data speaks: no durable edge
A clean 5-day sample: 10 trades, 2W–8L, +$0.83. The 0.66–0.72 shadow band that looked promising early regressed from 64% to 41% win rate as the sample grew — small-sample noise, not signal. Decision made: run untouched one more week, then shelve.
Aug 1
Discovered: stocks never traded once
2,958 stock signals over the whole run, all WATCH, zero BUY/SELL. Root cause: the scorer's stop/target sat at equal ATR distance in both directions, so risk/reward was mathematically always exactly 1.0 — and the stock path required 2.0. The "no edge" verdict was real for crypto; stocks were never actually tested.
Aug 5
Argus v2 shelved
Final position closed, every service stopped cleanly, code + database archived intact. With no live capital left at risk, the stock R/R gate and a sub-dollar rounding bug were fixed for the record, and a last research question — would a bigger target have done better? — was answered: no. This retrospective and the final CHANGELOG entry closed it out.

Honest Assessment
What Didn't Work
Core finding
No durable edge in the technical-scalp premise

The verdict, not a symptom: 21 trades, 33% win rate, net +$2.25. A promising early shadow-book band (0.66–0.72, +3.06R) regressed to break-even as the sample grew. The premise — RSI/EMA/MACD technical scoring on short holds — is too crowded for retail alpha at this scale.

Structural gap
Stocks never traded a single signal, the whole run

2,958 stock signals, zero BUY/SELL, for 20 days before anyone noticed. The scorer's own stop/target design made its R/R gate mathematically impossible to pass. The "no edge" verdict is honest only for crypto — stocks were silently never in the experiment.

Broker coverage
Forex and metals were analysis without execution

470 actionable forex/metal signals generated correctly and never once reached Alpaca — same gap as v1, still unsolved. The analytical layer works; the execution gateway for these asset classes was never built.

Untested feature
Shorting was built, enabled, and never fired

A full inverted-logic build — direction-aware stops, slot accounting, shortability checks, 53 tests green — armed for two weeks and never triggered once. Correctly built, but the live run never generated the data to know if it would have helped.

Rounding bug
Sub-dollar assets collapsed entry, stop, and target

A flat 2-decimal round made sub-dollar tickers (e.g. BAT, ~$0.20) print an identical price for entry, stop, and target. Minor in dollar terms, but a real correctness bug that sat live the entire run.

Small sample
21 trades is not enough to be certain of anything

The honest caveat: this record is suggestive, not statistically definitive. The decision to stop wasn't "we proved there's no edge" — it was "21 days of clean data plus a regressing shadow book is enough evidence to stop paying the opportunity cost of finding out for certain."

What Worked
Genuine Progress Made
Reliability
The system recovered from failure without a human

A chronic yfinance cache corruption used to need manual intervention. By July 19, the analyst detected it and re-exec'd itself — max unattended downtime dropped to about 5 minutes, watchdog-supervised, Telegram-narrated. No SSH required to stay alive.

High availability
A second machine actually stood guard

Agent 2's guardian, 10-minute replication, and warm standby ran for weeks without a real failover ever being needed — and when a genuine incident did hit (Jul 19, MacBook's own Tailscale daemon), the two-Mini pair was never the problem.

Real protection
Software stops did the job the broker couldn't

Crypto and fractional stock positions get no broker-side stop-loss from Alpaca. The 24/7 position monitor was the actual protection the whole run, and it never missed an exit.

Methodology
Every real decision was backed by a replay or a backtest

The trend veto (98-trade backtest), the 0.64 floor (edge analysis), the shorting build (53 tests before go-live), the final MFE check (163 signals) — nothing shipped on a hunch. When the data didn't support continuing, the system got shelved instead of tuned again.

Security
A full history rewrite, and it held

101 commits rewritten to scrub every private IP; verified zero credentials ever committed, before or after. The "no IPs in committed files" rule held for the rest of the run.

Closure
It ended on purpose, not by accident

No crash, no incident, no silent drift into abandonment. Final position closed, processes stopped, database archived and integrity-checked, two last bugs fixed for the record, everything logged and pushed — the same discipline that ran the system also closed it.

What v2 Proved, Beyond the Trade Record
This Was Run As an Experiment, Not a Hope

The most repeatable thing about Argus v2 wasn't any single feature — it was the discipline around how decisions got made. That process is the part that survives the shelving.

  • Two-book model

    Every untraded signal ≥0.62 was scored against real prices anyway, nightly, with zero real capital at risk — calibration data instead of guesswork about what a looser threshold would have done.

  • Decisions backed by replay

    The confidence floor, the trend veto, and the shorting build were all shipped after backtesting against real archived signals — not tuned live against the account balance.

  • Willing to see contrary evidence

    The shadow book's early 0.66–0.72 band looked like a real edge (+3.06R) until more data arrived and it regressed to 41%. That regression was reported and acted on, not explained away.

  • A final, independent check

    Before closing the book, the last open question — would a bigger target have caught bigger moves? — got tested on its own, after the verdict was already made. It confirmed the verdict instead of being skipped.

  • Stopping counted as a result

    "No edge, stop trading" was treated as a valid, useful outcome of the experiment — not a failure to route around with another threshold tweak.

"The system never lied to us. When the data said there wasn't an edge, we didn't tune around it — we closed the book. Two machines ran a real experiment for three weeks, recovered from their own failures, guarded each other, and told the truth about the result. That's a harder thing to build than a strategy that happens to work once."
